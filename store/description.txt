Two people, one photo. Tells measures what your bodies were doing in it — the gap, the angle of your shoulders, whether a hand was returned — using Apple's on-device pose model.

Pick a photo of the two of you, say which one is you, and get a read in seconds. Not a horoscope, not a personality test — just what two bodies were doing in one frame.

SEVEN SIGNALS, READ ONE BY ONE

• Space — the gap between your torsos, measured against shoulder width so it works at any zoom
• Angle — which way the shoulders are pointed. Turning your torso toward someone is a small commitment
• Touch — whether there's contact, who started it, and whether it was returned
• Face — the shape of the mouth: how far the corners lift, how wide it opens
• Eyes — where the gaze goes. The lens, each other, or away
• Sync — matching posture, matching lean. People copy the body language of people they're comfortable with
• Open — barriers. Crossed arms, a bag held between you, hands buried in pockets

WHAT YOU GET

A score out of 100, and a read that names it: Locked in, Green light, Slow burn, Mixed signals, Just friends, or Read the room.

A split. Not one number for "the vibe" — one for you and one for them. One-sided and mutual look identical in a thumbnail and mean completely different things.

The receipts. Three plain-English reasons for the score, ranked by how much they moved it. You can see the working.

A clarity rating. If the light was bad, the crop was tight or half the frame is missing, the app says so and marks the score as uncertain. It would rather tell you it isn't sure than make something up.

Set the context first — Crush, Situationship, Just met, Friend, Ex, or It's complicated — and the read is written for that, because the same photo means different things depending on what you two already are.

NOTHING LEAVES YOUR PHONE

Every part of the analysis runs on your device using Apple's own vision framework.

No account. No sign-up. No servers. No analytics. No advertising. No third-party code at all. Your photos are never uploaded, because there is nowhere to upload them to.

Turn on airplane mode and use the whole app. That's the actual test, and it works.

Your history stays on your phone and one tap in Settings erases all of it.

WHAT IT WON'T DO

This matters as much as the rest, so it's in the app too, in a section called "What we can't tell you".

Reading romantic interest from one photograph is not a validated method. Accuracy in the research on snap judgements tops out well short of certain, and that's with trained raters under lab conditions.

Body language shifts constantly. Whatever we read was true for the instant the shutter fired. Someone can be mid-laugh, mid-step, or just cold. How close people stand varies enormously between cultures, and we can't see where your photo was taken.

We'll tell you what their body did in the frame. We won't tell you what kind of person they are, what they want, or whether they're being honest with you. A photo can't answer that.

And the one that isn't negotiable: a score is never permission. Consent is something a person gives you in words, freely, and can take back at any time. Nothing this app produces changes that.

Another section explains all seven signals, what each is based on and how much weight it carries — including the ones researchers disagree about.

HOW IT'S BUILT

The signals come from published work on nonverbal behaviour: proxemics, facial action coding, and the courtship-signalling literature. Applying that to one still image is inference, not measurement, and the app says so on every result screen.

Every threshold in it was set by measuring real photographs, not by guessing. When the app can't read something, it tells you which signal it couldn't read and why, instead of filling the gap with a number.
